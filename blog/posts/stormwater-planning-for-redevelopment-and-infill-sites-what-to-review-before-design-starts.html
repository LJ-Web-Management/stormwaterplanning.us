Stormwater Planning for Redevelopment and Infill Sites: What to Review Before Design Starts

Redevelopment and infill projects often look easier than greenfield development because roads, utilities, buildings, and drainage systems already exist. In practice, those existing conditions can make stormwater planning more complicated. The practical question for owners, developers, civil engineers, municipalities, and plan reviewers is what should be checked before design starts so runoff, water quality, flood risk, maintenance, and permitting issues do not surface late in the project.

This question matters because infill sites are frequently constrained by small parcels, old pipe networks, undersized inlets, legacy grading, tight access, neighboring properties, and limited space for detention or green infrastructure. A stormwater management plan that ignores those constraints may lead to redesign, review delays, construction conflicts, or long-term maintenance problems.

Early planning does not promise permit approval, but it helps the team understand responsibilities before major design decisions are locked in.

Who This Affects

This issue affects developers considering older commercial parcels, municipalities reviewing infill proposals, public works departments managing existing drainage complaints, civil engineering firms preparing plans, property owners renovating large paved sites, industrial operators expanding outdoor areas, and builders working in dense corridors. It also affects planning departments, landscape architects, environmental compliance staff, and HOAs when redevelopment changes impervious cover or redirects flow.

Infill stormwater planning is especially important where new work connects to older municipal systems. The site may drain to a pipe that was not designed for current rainfall assumptions or upstream development. There may be combined sewer concerns, undersized culverts, poor outfall conditions, or maintenance responsibilities that are unclear. The project team needs to understand whether the site is adding runoff volume, changing peak discharge timing, affecting downstream properties, or triggering post-construction stormwater requirements.

Public works staff and plan reviewers may also have institutional knowledge that does not appear in a record drawing. They may know that an alley floods, a downstream inlet is difficult to clean, a ditch receives neighborhood complaints, or an outfall is near a sensitive receiving water. Bringing that context into the planning conversation early can help the design team avoid a technically polished plan that misses the practical operating reality of the local drainage system.

Property owners should care because stormwater decisions can affect budget and operations long after construction ends. A small underground detention system, proprietary treatment device, bioswale, permeable pavement area, or roof drain connection may come with inspection and maintenance obligations. Municipalities should care because one poorly planned redevelopment can add pressure to a drainage system that already receives complaints during routine storms.

The Stormwater Planning Issue

The core issue is that redevelopment sites are rarely blank slates. The existing drainage path may be partly visible and partly buried. Site records may be incomplete. Roof drains may connect to unknown pipes. Pavement may sheet flow to neighboring properties. Old inlets may be clogged, shallow, or connected to pipes with limited capacity. Nearby streets may already pond during storms.

Without early investigation, the stormwater plan can be based on assumptions rather than field conditions.

Regulatory context varies by state, municipality, watershed, and project size. Some projects may trigger construction stormwater permitting, erosion and sediment control planning, post-construction stormwater management, floodplain review, MS4-related requirements, or local drainage criteria.

Developers and owners should verify local requirements with the reviewing authority and qualified professionals. The planning value comes from identifying likely requirements early, not from assuming one universal rule applies everywhere.

In redevelopment, water quality and maintenance are often as important as peak flow. A site may need to reduce pollutants from parking areas, manage sediment during construction, protect nearby receiving waters, or document BMP maintenance responsibilities. Where space is limited, teams may compare underground storage, bioretention, planter systems, permeable pavement, proprietary treatment, rooftop controls, or off-site mitigation options if allowed locally. Each option has design, access, cost, and long-term maintenance implications.

Construction sequencing adds another layer. Older urban sites may have limited staging space, contaminated or compacted soils, active tenants, utility conflicts, and public sidewalks or streets that must remain open. Erosion and sediment control measures have to fit those constraints. If the team waits until mobilization to think through inlet protection, concrete washout, stockpile controls, dewatering, stabilized access, or street sweeping, the SWPPP and field setup may not match the actual work.

What To Review Before Moving Forward

A useful stormwater planning review starts with existing conditions. The team should gather surveys, utility records, record drawings, previous drainage studies if available, soil information, aerial imagery, flood maps, drainage complaint history, and local design criteria. Field observations are also important because maps may not show blocked inlets, broken curb lines, informal drainage paths, or ponding evidence.

  • Confirm existing and proposed impervious area.
  • Identify where roof, pavement, and landscaped areas currently drain.
  • Locate inlets, pipes, culverts, outfalls, ditches, and overflow paths.
  • Review downstream capacity concerns and known municipal drainage complaints.
  • Check whether the project triggers construction stormwater permit coverage or SWPPP planning.
  • Identify post- construction stormwater requirements and water quality standards.
  • Evaluate soil, groundwater, infiltration feasibility, and utility conflicts.
  • Review maintenance access for any proposed BMP, vault, filter, pond, or green infrastructure.
  • Clarify who will own, inspect, and maintain the stormwater controls after construction.

The review should also ask whether the proposed layout leaves room for stormwater controls. If building footprint, parking count, loading areas, drive aisles, landscaping, and utility corridors are fixed before stormwater is considered, the remaining options may be more expensive or less maintainable. Early stormwater planning gives the civil team a chance to shape the site plan instead of forcing stormwater into leftover spaces.

Owners should also ask how the chosen controls will be inspected after construction. A shallow landscaped BMP may need vegetation management, sediment removal, and protection from snow storage or vehicle damage. An underground system may need access ports, vacuum truck clearance, confined space considerations, and a maintenance schedule. These details affect site layout, agreements, and budgets. Reviewing them early helps avoid a plan that can be approved but is difficult to operate.

Planning, Permitting, or Compliance Support That Fits

Stormwaterplanning.us can support this type of project by helping owners, developers, municipalities, and design teams organize the stormwater questions before submittal. That may include stormwater management plan support, SWPPP planning, erosion and sediment control planning, drainage study coordination, BMP selection review, post-construction maintenance planning, MS4 program support, or documentation for plan review. The right service path depends on the project stage and the local requirements.

For a developer, the first need may be permit readiness: understanding what the reviewing agency will expect and what documentation the civil plans should include. For a municipality, the need may be a clearer review checklist or a way to connect redevelopment conditions to MS4 obligations. For a property owner, the need may be practical help documenting drainage conditions before committing to a purchase or renovation. For an industrial site, outdoor operations may also raise stormwater pollution prevention questions.

Careful planning does not guarantee approval or eliminate agency review. It can, however, reduce avoidable back-and-forth by making the stormwater basis of design clearer. It can also help the team choose controls that fit the site, can be built safely, and can be maintained after occupancy.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

A common mistake is waiting until final civil design to ask where stormwater controls will fit.

Another is relying only on desktop records without walking the site and checking drainage evidence.

Teams may also underestimate maintenance access, especially for underground systems or proprietary devices placed below parking areas. If inspection crews cannot reach the control, the design may create a long-term compliance problem.

Another mistake is treating redevelopment as automatically beneficial from a runoff standpoint. Some projects do improve drainage or water quality by replacing older pavement and adding controls, but others can increase impervious area, shift runoff, or overload a weak downstream system. Project teams should document the pre-development and post-development assumptions carefully. Municipal reviewers should also watch for overflow paths, tie-in elevations, and impacts to adjacent parcels.

Finally, teams should avoid making unsupported regulatory assumptions. Local thresholds, rainfall data, water quality criteria, detention requirements, and maintenance agreements can vary. Owners and developers should verify requirements early and update the plan when agency comments identify missing information.

Next Step

Before design starts, gather the existing drainage information, walk the site, review local criteria, and identify the stormwater controls that could realistically fit the project. That early review helps owners, developers, engineers, and municipal reviewers make better decisions before layouts harden and submittal deadlines arrive.

For redevelopment and infill sites, stormwater planning is not a decorative final page in the plan set. It is part of site feasibility, permit readiness, construction sequencing, and long-term maintenance. The next practical step is to clarify what the project changes, what the receiving system can handle, and what documentation the reviewing authority will need.
