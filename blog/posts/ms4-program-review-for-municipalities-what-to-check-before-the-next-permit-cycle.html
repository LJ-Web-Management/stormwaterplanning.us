MS4 Program Review for Municipalities: What to Check Before the Next Permit Cycle

Municipal stormwater staff often know their MS4 program needs attention long before the next permit cycle makes it urgent. Inspection records may be spread across departments. Outfall maps may be partly current and partly inherited from older GIS layers. Public education work may be happening, but not always in a way that clearly connects to measurable goals. Construction site oversight may depend on one experienced reviewer who knows the local process by memory. Good housekeeping tasks may be performed by public works crews every week, yet the records may not show what was done, where it happened, or how it supports the stormwater management program.

That is the practical question behind an MS4 program review: what should a municipality check before permit renewal, annual reporting, audits, staff turnover, or a major program update?

For city managers, public works directors, engineers, planners, and compliance coordinators, the value of an MS4 program review is not limited to finding gaps. A useful review turns scattered program activity into a clearer operating system. It helps the community understand which responsibilities belong to engineering, which belong to public works, which require planning or zoning coordination, and which need support from elected officials or finance staff. It also gives developers, contractors, and property owners a more predictable process because local expectations become easier to explain.

The review does not need to promise a perfect program. Stormwater responsibilities shift as land use changes, permits are reissued, assets age, and staffing changes. The goal is to understand the current program well enough to make defensible improvements, update documentation, and focus limited resources on the activities most likely to reduce pollutant discharge, improve maintenance decisions, and support compliance planning.

Who This Affects

An MS4 program review is most useful for regulated municipal separate storm sewer system operators, but the effects reach far beyond the person who files the annual report.

Public works directors are often responsible for day-to-day maintenance, drainage complaint response, street sweeping, inlet cleaning, winter operations, material storage, and municipal facility housekeeping. If the program review does not include those operational realities, the written stormwater management program can drift away from how work is actually performed.

City and county engineers need the review because construction site runoff control, post-construction stormwater management, drainage standards, BMP acceptance, as-built review, and capital project design all depend on clear local expectations. Engineering staff may also hold the best information about recurring flooding, undersized conveyance, failed outfalls, redevelopment constraints, and assets that should be prioritized for mapping or inspection.

Planning and zoning departments are affected because many MS4 responsibilities intersect with development review. Site plan conditions, subdivision standards, redevelopment incentives, green infrastructure allowances, parking lot drainage, easements, and long-term maintenance agreements all shape whether stormwater controls are practical after construction.

Building officials, code enforcement staff, parks departments, fleet managers, facility managers, streets crews, and finance staff also have a role. They may not think of their daily work as stormwater program implementation, but municipal operations can affect runoff quality and documentation quality. A salt storage area, wash bay, fueling area, compost pile, or maintenance yard can become important during an MS4 review.

Developers, builders, civil engineering firms, HOAs, commercial property owners, and industrial property operators are affected indirectly. A clearer municipal program can make submittal requirements easier to understand, reduce inconsistent review comments, improve long-term BMP maintenance expectations, and clarify who is responsible for drainage infrastructure after construction.

The Stormwater Planning Issue

EPA describes regulated MS4 operators as entities that must develop, implement, and enforce a stormwater management program. For small MS4s, the core program areas commonly include public education and outreach, public involvement and participation, illicit discharge detection and elimination, construction site runoff control, post-construction runoff control, and pollution prevention or good housekeeping for municipal operations. Permit details vary by state, permit authority, watershed, impaired waters requirements, and local conditions, so a municipality should always review its own permit language before making decisions.

The planning issue is that many MS4 programs grow in pieces. A community may have adopted an ordinance years ago, added a construction inspection form later, built a partial storm drain inventory with grant funding, and created public education materials for one annual event. Each action may be reasonable by itself. The problem is that the pieces may not tell one complete story.

That story matters during permit renewal, annual reporting, staff transition, audits, budget requests, and public discussions about drainage needs. If the city says it has an illicit discharge program, can staff show the current outfall inventory, screening schedule, complaint response procedure, investigation notes, and follow-up process? If the city requires post-construction BMP maintenance, can it identify the privately maintained BMPs, responsible parties, maintenance agreement locations, and inspection history? If public works is cleaning catch basins, can the program explain how cleaning locations are selected and how the work is recorded?

An MS4 review should also account for the difference between compliance paperwork and useful management information. A program can have binders, forms, and annual reports without giving leadership a clear picture of risk, workload, or priorities. Conversely, crews may be doing solid field work without enough documentation to explain the work later. The strongest review connects both sides: what the permit requires and how the municipality actually operates.

The review should be careful with regulatory claims. It should not assume that every community has the same obligations, that every BMP example is required, or that every missing document means noncompliance. Instead, it should compare the local permit, stormwater management program, ordinances, procedures, inspection records, mapping, and annual reports against each other. When those materials disagree or leave gaps, the municipality has a practical improvement target.

What To Review Before Moving Forward

Start with the current permit and the written stormwater management program. The permit is the controlling document, but the SWMP usually explains how the municipality intends to carry out permit requirements. Check whether the written program still matches current staffing, departments, ordinances, field practices, and reporting commitments.

  • List each permit requirement and the local procedure that supports it.
  • Identify the responsible department or staff role for each program element.
  • Compare annual report commitments against records from the same year.
  • Note activities that happen in practice but are not documented well.
  • Note written procedures that are no longer being followed.
  • Flag items that depend on one staff member's memory instead of a repeatable process.

Review the six common minimum control measure areas as separate program tracks, then look for connections between them.

  1. Public education and outreach

Check whether education activities are tied to actual local stormwater issues. A generic brochure may have less value than targeted outreach about yard waste, pet waste, construction sediment, vehicle fluids, fertilizer use, or private BMP maintenance. Review the audience, message, distribution method, timing, and documentation. If the municipality reports education activity, it should be able to show what was provided, who it was intended to reach, and how it supports the program.

  1. Public involvement and participation

Look at how residents, businesses, and community groups can participate in stormwater decisions or activities. This may include public meetings, cleanups, reporting channels, watershed events, advisory committees, volunteer monitoring, or comment opportunities. The review should check whether participation is accessible, documented, and connected to local priorities rather than treated as a one-time annual task.

  1. Illicit discharge detection and elimination

Review the storm sewer map, outfall inventory, dry-weather screening records, complaint procedures, investigation steps, enforcement tools, and follow-up records. The municipality should understand where known outfalls are located, which areas need additional mapping, how suspicious flows are handled, and how staff distinguish routine drainage complaints from potential illicit discharge concerns. Coordination with sanitary sewer, code enforcement, fire, health, and industrial pretreatment staff may be important.

  1. Construction site runoff control

Check ordinances, plan review procedures, inspection forms, enforcement steps, active project lists, complaint response, and closeout documentation. The review should ask whether reviewers and inspectors use consistent expectations for erosion and sediment controls, stabilization, inlet protection, perimeter controls, concrete washout, construction entrances, and phasing. It should also check whether local requirements align with state construction stormwater permit responsibilities without implying that local review replaces state permit coverage.

  1. Post-construction runoff control

Review how permanent stormwater controls are selected, approved, recorded, inspected, and maintained. This is where many programs develop long-term risk. A pond, bioretention cell, infiltration practice, proprietary structure, or underground detention system may be approved during development review, but the municipality also needs clarity about ownership, access, easements, maintenance standards, inspection frequency, and corrective action. The program review should check whether private and public BMPs are visible in the asset system and whether maintenance responsibilities survive property transfers.

  1. Pollution prevention and good housekeeping

Walk through municipal operations with practical eyes. Review fleet maintenance, public works yards, salt and sand storage, street sweeping, catch basin cleaning, pesticide and fertilizer use, parks maintenance, spill response, material stockpiles, dumpsters, vehicle washing, fueling, and training. The point is not to create paperwork for its own sake. The point is to reduce pollutant exposure and make routine work easier to document.

The next layer is data. A good MS4 review should ask what information the municipality trusts, what information is incomplete, and what information staff need for decisions.

  • Current MS4 boundary and urbanized area information
  • Storm sewer system map, including pipes, inlets, manholes, ditches, swales, culverts, and outfalls where available
  • Receiving waters, impaired waters, TMDL-related obligations, and priority watersheds
  • Active construction sites and recent closeouts
  • Publicly owned BMPs and privately owned BMPs
  • Drainage complaint locations and recurring maintenance calls
  • Municipal facilities and operations with stormwater exposure
  • Training records, inspection records, enforcement records, and annual report backup
  • Capital improvement needs tied to stormwater assets or flood mitigation

Finally, review the program from the user's point of view. A developer should be able to understand what stormwater documentation is expected before submittal. A resident should know how to report dumping or drainage concerns. A crew leader should know what form or system to use after cleaning inlets. A reviewer should be able to explain why a post-construction BMP requires an access easement or maintenance agreement. If these basic interactions are unclear, the program may need process work as much as technical work.

Planning, Permitting, or Compliance Support That Fits

Stormwater Planning can support an MS4 program review by helping organize the practical pieces that connect permit language to municipal operations. That support may include reviewing the current stormwater management program, comparing annual report commitments with available records, evaluating ordinance and plan review procedures, organizing construction and post-construction workflows, identifying mapping and asset inventory gaps, and preparing a prioritized action list for staff discussion.

For municipalities, the strongest support is often a working review that produces usable decisions. That means separating urgent documentation gaps from longer-term program improvements. It means identifying which updates require legal counsel, which require engineering judgment, which require public works procedure changes, and which are mostly recordkeeping or training issues. It also means avoiding broad claims that one checklist can solve every permit concern.

For developers and civil engineering teams, MS4 review support can clarify how local expectations affect submittals. A city that has recently reviewed its program may be better positioned to explain what must be included in civil plans, how long-term BMP maintenance will be documented, what inspections are expected during construction, and how closeout information should be delivered.

For property owners, HOAs, and facility managers, the review may reveal where private maintenance responsibilities need clearer records. Older developments may have stormwater ponds, underground chambers, swales, or outlet structures with incomplete documentation. Identifying those responsibilities early can reduce confusion when drainage complaints, redevelopment, or enforcement questions arise.

The service path is practical: understand the permit and program, organize records, inspect the workflow, identify gaps, and help staff choose next steps. That can support compliance planning and reduce review delays, but it should never be framed as a guarantee of permit approval, audit findings, funding, or regulatory acceptance.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

One common mistake is treating the annual report as the program. Annual reports are important, but they are summaries of work and commitments. The operating program lives in procedures, maps, staff responsibilities, inspection records, ordinances, maintenance practices, and communication between departments. If those pieces are weak, the annual report will be harder to support.

Another mistake is reviewing only the engineering files. MS4 implementation often depends on public works, planning, code enforcement, parks, fleet, facilities, finance, communications, and administration. A review that stays inside one department may miss the places where stormwater responsibilities are actually carried out.

A third mistake is assuming the storm sewer map is complete enough because a GIS layer exists. Many communities have useful GIS data, but the layer may not include private connections, unmapped ditches, old culverts, field-verified outfalls, abandoned infrastructure, or recent development changes. The review should distinguish between mapped, field-verified, assumed, and unknown assets.

Municipalities also run into trouble when post-construction BMPs are approved without a durable maintenance trail. A BMP that looks acceptable at plan review can become a long-term issue if ownership, access, inspection expectations, and maintenance funding are unclear. The review should check whether the municipality can find the maintenance agreement, identify the responsible party, access the facility, and document follow-up.

Construction site oversight can become inconsistent when review comments, inspection findings, and enforcement steps are not connected. If an erosion control issue appears repeatedly, staff should be able to see whether it is a plan quality problem, contractor sequencing problem, inspection frequency problem, or local standard problem. Without that connection, the municipality may keep reacting to the same issue one site at a time.

Another mistake is relying on informal staff knowledge without converting it into procedure. Experienced staff are valuable, but turnover can expose gaps quickly. A program review should capture who does what, where records are stored, how complaints are routed, how inspections are scheduled, how enforcement escalates, and how annual reporting backup is assembled.

Finally, avoid building a review that produces only a long deficiency list. Staff need priorities. A useful review should separate immediate documentation needs, process updates, mapping tasks, ordinance questions, training needs, and capital planning issues. That makes the result easier to act on and easier to explain to leadership.

Next Step

Before the next permit cycle, audit, annual report, or major staff transition, gather the current permit, SWMP, annual reports, ordinances, inspection forms, storm sewer maps, construction and post-construction records, municipal facility information, and drainage complaint history. Then compare what the program says it does with what staff can actually document.

The best first step is not a perfect rewrite. It is a clear inventory of responsibilities, records, gaps, and priorities. From there, a municipality can update procedures, focus mapping work, improve development review consistency, strengthen BMP maintenance tracking, and prepare better support for future budget or staffing discussions. A practical MS4 review gives the community a more reliable stormwater program and gives staff a clearer path for the next round of work.
