## CATEGORY: SWPPP AND TRAINING BASICS

Q: What is a SWPPP?
A: A Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) is a site-specific document that
   identifies how a construction project will control erosion, manage sediment, and
   prevent pollutants from entering nearby waterways during and after construction. It
   includes a site map, potential pollutant sources, Best Management Practices (BMPs),
   and inspection/maintenance procedures. Also called SWP3, SW3P, SWMP, CBMPP, or ESCP
   depending on the state/agency. Traces back to the Clean Water Act and EPA's NPDES
   Construction General Permit.

Q: What is stormwater training for construction, and why does it matter?
A: It teaches site personnel, supervisors, and inspectors to recognize pollution risks,
   install/maintain BMPs, complete required inspections, and keep the SWPPP updated.
   Untrained crews are the most common source of permit violations, fines, and
   preventable environmental damage.

Q: Who needs stormwater or SWPPP training?
A: Anyone with day-to-day responsibility for erosion and sediment control: general
   contractors and site superintendents; subcontractors performing grading, excavation,
   or utility work; designated stormwater inspectors and Qualified SWPPP Practitioners
   (QSPs); environmental consultants and municipal construction inspectors; project
   owners and developers who hold the permit.

Q: What topics does stormwater training typically cover?
A: Overview of stormwater regulations; how to read/apply a SWPPP; BMPs for erosion and
   sediment control; inspection and sampling procedures; spill response and good
   housekeeping; recordkeeping for permit compliance. stormwaterplanning.us splits these
   by role: QSP Practitioner (field implementation) vs SWPPP Preparer (plan preparation).

## CATEGORY: PERMITS, REGULATIONS AND LEGAL REQUIREMENTS

Q: Is stormwater training legally required on construction sites?
A: Yes, in most cases. Sites disturbing one acre or more of soil (or part of a larger
   common plan of development that does) must obtain NPDES or state construction
   stormwater permit coverage, which typically requires "qualified personnel" to
   prepare, implement, and inspect under the SWPPP.

Q: What size project needs a SWPPP?
A: Under EPA rules, any construction activity disturbing one acre or more of land
   requires SWPPP coverage. Smaller phased projects may still need one if part of a
   larger common plan of development totaling one acre or more.

Q: Who is responsible for developing and maintaining the SWPPP?
A: The project owner or primary permittee (often the general contractor), even if
   drafting is delegated to a qualified professional or trained staff. Responsibility
   for compliance cannot be fully outsourced away from the permit holder.

Q: How often are stormwater inspections required?
A: Most state programs require inspections every 7-14 days, plus an additional
   inspection within 24 hours of a qualifying rain event (commonly 0.5" or more).
   Frequency and thresholds vary by state.

Q: What happens if a site is found out of compliance?
A: Significant fines (often tens of thousands of dollars per day per violation
   depending on the state), stop-work orders, legal action, and reputational damage.
   Regulators can require the SWPPP to be revised and re-implemented.

Q: When does a SWPPP need to be updated?
A: Any time site design, construction sequencing, operations, or maintenance changes in
   a way that could affect pollutant discharge, or when inspections show existing BMPs
   aren't working. Covered in depth in the SWPPP Preparer course.

Q: What is the difference between a SWPPP and stormwater training?
A: A SWPPP is the written, site-specific compliance document required by a construction
   permit. Stormwater training is the education that equips personnel to write,
   implement, inspect, and maintain that SWPPP correctly. A site can have a SWPPP on
   file and still fail inspections if the crew implementing it is untrained.

Q: Can stormwater training be customized for a specific state or region?
A: Yes. Because rules are implemented at the state/municipal level, many providers pair
   a baseline federal course (e.g. EPA's national program) with a state/local supplement
   covering regional thresholds, inspection intervals, and reporting forms.
   stormwaterplanning.us courses are built on the federal EPA/NPDES framework; confirm
   any state-specific supplement separately.

SWPPP & Stormwater Training Basics

A Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) is a site-specific document that identifies how a construction project will control erosion, manage sediment, and prevent pollutants from entering nearby waterways during and after construction. It includes a site map, a list of potential pollutant sources, Best Management Practices (BMPs), and inspection and maintenance procedures.

Depending on the state or permitting agency, the same document may also be called a SWP3, SW3P, Stormwater Management Plan (SWMP), Construction Best Management Practices Plan (CBMPP), or Erosion and Sediment Control Plan (ESCP). The requirement traces back to the federal Clean Water Act and the EPA's NPDES Construction General Permit.

Stormwater training for construction teaches site personnel, supervisors, and inspectors how to recognize pollution risks, install and maintain BMPs, complete required inspections, and keep the SWPPP updated as site conditions change. It matters because untrained crews are the most common source of permit violations, fines, and preventable environmental damage.

Anyone with day-to-day responsibility for erosion and sediment control needs training, including general contractors, site superintendents, subcontractors who disturb soil, stormwater inspectors, and any "qualified personnel" named in the site's permit. Many state permits specifically require the person conducting inspections to hold a recognized certification:

  • General contractors and site superintendents
  • Subcontractors performing grading, excavation, or utility work
  • Designated stormwater inspectors and Qualified SWPPP Practitioners (QSPs)
  • Environmental consultants and municipal construction inspectors
  • Project owners and developers who hold the permit

Most construction stormwater courses cover the same core areas: an overview of stormwater regulations, how to read and apply a SWPPP, Best Management Practices for erosion and sediment control, inspection and sampling procedures, spill response and good housekeeping, and recordkeeping requirements for permit compliance. Our own QSP Practitioner and SWPPP Preparer courses split these topics by role: field implementation versus plan preparation.

Regulatory Terms Explained

A certificate of completion, issued immediately after passing the course, QSP Practitioner Training certificates are valid 24 months, SWPPP Preparer Training certificates are valid 36 months. It documents training content completed; it is not a government-issued license. See Certificate Policy.

QSP (Qualified SWPPP Practitioner) implements and inspects the SWPPP in the field. QSD (Qualified SWPPP Developer) develops and revises the SWPPP document itself. Both are formal California state qualifications administered through CASQA. See QSP vs. QSD Training.

"Qualified person" is EPA's federal CGP term for whoever the permittee designates to perform inspections, there's no formal EPA certification process. "QSP" is California's formal, state-administered credential with its own prerequisites and exam. See Qualified Person vs. QSP.

A Construction General Permit (CGP) is the NPDES permit covering stormwater discharges from construction sites disturbing one acre or more. EPA issues a CGP directly in a few jurisdictions (Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Mexico, DC, most territories/tribal lands); most states run their own CGP. See epa.gov/npdes/construction-general-permit-cgp.

The National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) is the Clean Water Act permitting program that regulates point-source discharges, including construction stormwater. See epa.gov/npdes.

Section 402 is the part of the Clean Water Act that establishes the NPDES permitting program, the legal basis for construction stormwater permits and SWPPP requirements.

40 CFR 122.26 is the federal regulation implementing NPDES stormwater discharge permitting, including construction activity. See ecfr.gov.

Best Management Practices (BMPs) are the erosion-control, sediment-control, and pollution-prevention measures used to keep a construction site in compliance, silt fencing, inlet protection, stabilized entrances, concrete washout areas, and more. See Stormwater BMP Training.

Numeric Action Levels (NALs) and Numeric Effluent Limits (NELs) are specific pollutant concentration thresholds that can appear in some state Construction General Permits (notably California's). Exceeding an NAL typically triggers additional BMPs or reporting; exceeding an NEL can trigger stricter corrective action or enforcement. Our SWPPP Preparer course covers monitoring and numeric limits as part of its curriculum.
